Two treatments, two different jobs

Both are minimally invasive injectables. The difference is what they change: Botox softens movement, fillers restore volume.

Botox

A purified protein (botulinum toxin type A) that temporarily relaxes the small muscles that create expression lines.

Best for
Frown lines, forehead lines and crow's feet.
When you see results
Usually within 3 to 7 days, settling fully at about 2 weeks.
How long it lasts
Typically 3 to 4 months, then muscle movement gradually returns.

Dermal fillers

Soft gels, most often hyaluronic acid, placed under the skin to add volume, smooth folds and shape features.

Best for
Cheeks, lips, smile lines, chin and jawline.
When you see results
Immediately, with any swelling settling over a few days.
How long it lasts
Roughly 6 to 18 months, depending on the product and area. Hyaluronic acid fillers can be dissolved if needed.

    What to expect

    A typical visit is short, and nothing happens on the day unless you are comfortable with the plan.

    1. Consultation

      Your clinician reviews your health history, listens to your goals and explains the options and risks.

    2. Plan

      Together you agree on what to treat, and what to leave alone. Botox is a prescription treatment, so this step is required.

    3. Treatment

      Small injections, usually 15 to 30 minutes. Numbing cream or built-in anaesthetic keeps fillers comfortable.

    4. Aftercare

      Most people return to normal activity straight away. Mild swelling or bruising is common and short-lived.

    Qualifications and safety

    Rules on who can inject in England are still evolving. These are the standards to look for in any practitioner.

    A prescriber assesses you first

    Botox is a prescription-only medicine. It must be prescribed by a registered doctor, dentist, or nurse or pharmacist independent prescriber (registered with the GMC, GDC, NMC or GPhC) after assessing you.

    Specialist injector training

    A Level 7 (postgraduate-level) qualification in botulinum toxin and dermal fillers is the widely recognised benchmark. Ask which body awarded it and when.

    Professional registration

    Check your practitioner on the relevant professional register, and whether they belong to a voluntary register such as JCCP or Save Face.

    Insurance and emergency readiness

    Ask about professional indemnity insurance and training in managing complications, including having hyaluronidase available for filler treatments.

    Fillers need extra care in choosing

    Dermal fillers are medical devices, not prescription medicines, and at the time of writing the law does not restrict who may inject them. Checking training matters even more.

    Adults only

    In England it is illegal to give Botox or cosmetic fillers to anyone under 18 for cosmetic purposes.

    Common questions

    Will I look frozen or overfilled?

    Not if the treatment is planned conservatively. The aim is a rested, natural version of you, and a good clinician will adjust doses and amounts to keep movement and proportions natural.

    Does it hurt?

    Most people describe brief pinching. Fillers often contain a local anaesthetic, and numbing cream can be used for more sensitive areas like the lips.

    What are the side effects?

    Common ones are redness, swelling, tenderness and bruising, which usually fade within days. Serious complications are rare, which is why treatment should always be carried out by a properly qualified, insured practitioner.

    Can I have both at once?

    Yes. Many people combine them, using Botox to soften lines and filler to restore volume. Your clinician will advise what suits your face.

    Who should not have these treatments?

    Treatment is generally avoided during pregnancy and breastfeeding, and if you have an active skin infection or certain neuromuscular conditions. Tell your clinician about your full medical history and any medication you take.
